# Waren's Style — Story-Driven Songwriting Workflow

> **Optional workflow example:** This is one songwriter's chosen method, included to make the plugin adaptable. Use it only when the current user asks for this approach or their confirmed project preferences select it. Other users can choose a different writing style, process, and collaboration pace. The plugin's default is to discover and follow the current songwriter's preferences.

## Purpose

Create original, emotionally sincere, memorable songs using a collaborative, section-by-section songwriting process.

The songwriting approach combines:

- **Direct romantic storytelling:** Simple, honest, conversational language that feels like something a real person would say.
- **Emotional progression:** Every section moves the story forward instead of repeating the same idea.
- **Memorable hooks:** Catchy, singable choruses with natural repetition and clear emotional meaning.
- **Everyday imagery:** Familiar situations and relatable details rather than excessive poetry.
- **Musical precision:** Consistent syllable counts, natural phrasing, strong rhymes, and melodies that are comfortable to sing.

The goal is to make listeners understand the story immediately while leaving them with a chorus they remember.

## 1. Understand the Story Before Writing

Before generating lyrics, establish:

1. Who is telling the story?
2. Who is the song about?
3. What happened in the past?
4. What is happening in the present?
5. What does the narrator wish had happened?
6. What can no longer be changed?
7. What emotion should the listener feel at the end?

Identify the central emotional conflict.

Do not immediately write the full song.

Discuss the story with the songwriter until the emotional direction is clear.

## 2. Write the Chorus First

**The chorus is the emotional destination of the song.**

Start by writing the chorus before any verses.

The chorus should:

- Express the main emotional conflict.
- Contain a memorable, repeatable hook.
- Use direct, understandable language.
- Be easy to sing.
- Have consistent rhythmic phrasing.
- Use natural rhymes without sacrificing meaning.
- Work even when heard without the verses.

A chorus may contain two emotional movements:

**First half: Desire or imagination**

What does the narrator wish were possible?

**Second half: Reality or acceptance**

What prevents that wish from becoming reality?

### Example: A Little Too Late

The chorus begins with imagined possibilities:

"I wish I'd built a home for you"

Then acknowledges the narrator's present commitment:

"But now there's someone by my side"

Finally, it resolves the emotional conflict:

"For us, it's a little too late"

This creates a complete emotional arc within the chorus.

### Collaboration rule

Present chorus drafts, discuss the wording, and revise them until the songwriter explicitly approves.

Once approved, treat the chorus as locked unless the songwriter requests changes.

## 3. Write the Bridge Second

**The bridge reveals the deeper truth behind the song.**

After finalizing the chorus, write the bridge before the verses.

The bridge should answer:

- What is the narrator finally realizing?
- What is the emotional truth that hasn't been expressed yet?
- What decision or acceptance changes the meaning of the chorus?

The bridge must contribute something new.

Avoid simply restating the chorus.

### Example: A Little Too Late

The narrator wishes he could help people who have been hurt, but recognizes he cannot save everyone.

The bridge then reveals his commitment to his current partner:

"But you're the one I want to hold"

This transforms the song from simple romantic regret into a story about maturity, responsibility, and accepting the present.

### Bridge structure

A useful eight-line bridge can follow this progression:

- Lines 1–2: Introduce the deeper emotional problem.
- Lines 3–4: Acknowledge personal limitations.
- Lines 5–6: Reveal what truly matters.
- Lines 7–8: Deliver the emotional decision or resolution.

Keep the language sincere and conversational.

## 4. Write the Pre-Chorus Third

**The pre-chorus is the emotional ladder leading into the chorus.**

It connects the verses to the central emotional statement.

Its job is to create anticipation and explain why the chorus follows.

The pre-chorus should:

- Reveal an unresolved thought, confession, or regret.
- Increase emotional tension.
- Avoid repeating the chorus's conclusion.
- End with a line that naturally leads into the chorus.

### Example: A Little Too Late

"I never told you how I feel"

followed by:

"If I had spoken up that day"

The pre-chorus establishes the missed opportunity, making the chorus's repeated "I wish" feel emotionally justified.

### Important rule

Do not invent events that contradict the story.

If the narrator never spoke to the person, do not write lyrics implying they had a relationship or exchanged confessions.

Narrative consistency matters more than a convenient rhyme.

## 5. Write Verse 1 — The Past

**Verse 1 establishes the emotional beginning.**

Show what life felt like before the conflict developed.

For romantic songs, this may include:

- The first attraction.
- Small everyday moments.
- Innocent excitement.
- Unspoken feelings.
- Memories that later become meaningful.

### Example: A Little Too Late

"Every time I saw you smile"

The narrator remembers admiring someone from a distance.

The verse feels warm and innocent, creating contrast with the later sadness.

### Rule

Do not reveal the entire story in Verse 1.

Give the listener a reason to care before introducing the conflict.

## 6. Write Verse 2 — The Present

**Verse 2 shows what changed.**

Move the story forward in time.

Avoid rewriting Verse 1 with different words.

Possible developments include:

- Discovering someone's current situation.
- Seeing how time has changed a relationship.
- Recognizing consequences.
- Experiencing regret or disappointment.
- Comparing past expectations with present reality.

### Example: A Little Too Late

"I see the pictures that you share"

The narrator now observes the woman's life through social media.

"The smile I used to love is gone"

This connects directly to the smile introduced in Verse 1.

### Important technique: Emotional callbacks

Reuse a meaningful image or detail from Verse 1, but give it a different emotional context in Verse 2.

This makes the story feel connected and intentional.

## 7. Refine Syllables, Rhymes, and Singability

After writing each section, review every line.

### Syllable consistency

Prefer similar syllable counts across lines that share a melodic pattern.

Eight syllables per line can be a useful starting point, but it is not mandatory.

Natural singing rhythm matters more than mechanically forcing every line to match.

### Rhyming

Prefer clear, natural end rhymes.

Examples:

- smile / while
- place / face
- side / aside
- pain / remain
- fate / late

Avoid awkward grammar, unusual word order, or meaningless filler added only to make a rhyme work.

### Singability

Read each line aloud.

Check whether:

- The words flow naturally.
- The emotional emphasis falls on important words.
- The singer can pronounce the phrase comfortably.
- The line fits the intended melody.
- The language sounds conversational rather than artificially poetic.

### Quality rule

A simple, emotionally accurate line is better than a complicated, impressive-sounding line that feels unnatural.

## 8. Use an Iterative Approval Process

Work on one section at a time.

For each section:

1. Present a draft.
2. Explain its emotional purpose when useful.
3. Listen to the songwriter's feedback.
4. Revise only what needs changing.
5. Check rhymes, syllables, and story consistency.
6. Request approval before proceeding.

When the songwriter says "Perfect," "Approved," or otherwise confirms a section, treat it as finalized.

Do not silently rewrite approved sections.

If the songwriter rejects a line, identify the specific issue instead of rewriting the entire song unnecessarily.

The songwriter retains creative authority.

## 9. Arrange the Complete Song Last

Only after the chorus, bridge, pre-chorus, and verses have been approved should the complete arrangement be assembled.

A recommended structure is:

Intro → Verse 1 → Pre-Chorus → Chorus → Instrumental Break → Verse 2 → Pre-Chorus → Chorus → Bridge → Chorus → Final Chorus → Outro

The arrangement can change depending on the song.

### Chorus teaser intro

A short excerpt of the chorus can be placed at the beginning to establish the hook immediately.

### Final chorus

The final chorus can feature additional energy, backing vocals, harmonies, and ad-libs without changing the original lead lyrics.

Use parentheses to distinguish backing vocal responses.

Example:

"I wish you'd loved me like I do (I do!)"

Keep the backing vocals complementary to the lead rather than creating an unintended duet.

## 10. Separate Songwriting from Music Production

**Lyrics tell the story. Production determines how the story sounds.**

Do not rewrite finalized lyrics merely because the genre, instrumentation, tempo, or mix direction changes.

Instead, update:

- The main Suno Style of Music prompt.
- Excluded Styles.
- Section-level production instructions.
- Vocal delivery instructions.
- Instrumentation and arrangement notes.

For example, the same lyrics can move from dreamy electropop to trap-pop while preserving their original emotional meaning.

### Suno section instructions

Place musical direction inside square-bracketed section headers.

Example:

[Chorus – Catchy Electropop Hook, Energetic Trap-Pop Drums, Deep 808 Bass, Sparkling Synths, Clear Male Lead Vocals]

Use these instructions to describe arrangement and delivery, not to replace the actual lyrics.

## 11. Deliver Complete Suno Materials

When the songwriter requests "Suno materials," provide:

1. **Style of Music:** A complete production prompt within 1,000 characters.
2. **Excluded Styles:** Instruments, genres, or vocal treatments to avoid.
3. **Complete Lyrics:** All approved lyrics with detailed Suno section instructions.
4. **Suggested Settings:** Weirdness, Style Influence, Audio Influence when applicable, and other relevant controls.

Preserve all approved lyrics.

Keep the main Style prompt and individual section instructions consistent.

## 12. Final Quality Checklist

Before delivering the song, verify:

- The chorus communicates the main emotional conflict.
- The bridge introduces a meaningful realization.
- The pre-chorus naturally leads into the chorus.
- Verse 1 establishes the past.
- Verse 2 shows meaningful change.
- The narrative contains no contradictions.
- The lyrics use sincere, understandable language.
- Rhymes sound natural.
- Lines are comfortable to sing.
- Approved lyrics remain unchanged.
- Production instructions support the intended musical identity.

## Core Philosophy

**Chorus first. Bridge second. Pre-chorus third. Verses afterward. Arrangement last.**

Start with what the narrator feels, discover why they feel it, and then tell the story that brings the listener to the same emotional conclusion.

Write with honesty, not complexity.

Prioritize meaning over rhyme, singability over rigid syllable counts, and emotional progression over decorative language.

The final song should sound simple enough to remember, natural enough to believe, and meaningful enough to stay with the listener.
